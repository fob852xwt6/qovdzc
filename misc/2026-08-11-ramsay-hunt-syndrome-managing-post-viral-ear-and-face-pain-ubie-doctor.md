Ramsay Hunt Syndrome: Managing Post-Viral Ear and Face Pain

Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Internal Medicine

Ramsay Hunt syndrome develops when the dormant varicella-zoster virus reactivates within the facial and vestibulocochlear nerves. Common symptoms include sharp ear pain, painful blisters on the ear or inside the mouth, facial weakness or paralysis on one side, and sometimes hearing loss, tinnitus, vertigo, or balance problems.

Early treatment is critical. Doctors typically prescribe antiviral medications (such as acyclovir or valacyclovir), corticosteroids to reduce nerve inflammation, pain relievers, and eye lubrication or patching to protect the eye when the eyelid cannot close fully. Rehabilitation, including facial exercises and physical therapy, may support recovery. Warning signs requiring urgent care include worsening paralysis, severe dizziness, sudden hearing loss, or eye irritation.

Because Ramsay Hunt syndrome shares symptoms with Bell's palsy, stroke, and inner ear conditions, identifying the cause quickly matters. Starting antivirals within 72 hours of symptom onset significantly improves the chance of full facial recovery. What Is Ramsay Hunt Syndrome?

Caused by reactivation of the varicella-zoster virus (the same virus behind chickenpox and shingles).

Affects the facial (VII) and sometimes the vestibulocochlear (VIII) nerves.

Presents with ear pain, a painful blistering rash on the ear or mouth, and facial weakness or paralysis on one side.

Common Symptoms

Shingles pain in the ear: Often sharp, stabbing or burning.

Facial paralysis: Difficulty closing the eye or smiling on one side.

Rash and blisters: Small fluid-filled blisters on the ear, behind it, or inside the mouth.

Hearing changes: Tinnitus (ringing), hearing loss, or hyperacusis (sensitivity to sound).

Why Does Shingles Pain in the Ear Occur?

When varicella-zoster virus reactivates, it inflames the nerves it resides in. In Ramsay Hunt syndrome:

The facial nerve controls facial muscles and some taste sensation.

The nearby vestibulocochlear nerve affects hearing and balance.

Inflammation of these nerves leads to pain, rash, and functional changes.

Shingles pain in the ear can be intense because the ear canal and surrounding skin have many nerve endings. Early treatment is key to reducing nerve damage and pain.

Medical Treatments

Prompt medical intervention can speed recovery and limit complications:

Antiviral medications (e.g., acyclovir, valacyclovir)• Shorten the duration of rash and pain• Reduce nerve damage

Corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone)• Decrease nerve inflammation• Often given alongside antivirals

Pain relief• Over-the-counter analgesics (acetaminophen, ibuprofen)• Prescription nerve pain medicines (gabapentin, pregabalin)• Topical lidocaine patches for localized ear pain

Eye protection• Lubricating drops or ointment if eyelid closure is weak• Eyepatch at night to prevent corneal injury

Your doctor will tailor dosages based on severity, age, and overall health.

Self-Care and Home Management

Even with medical treatment, self-care helps control discomfort and support healing:

Rest and manage stress: Give your body time to recover.

Warm compresses: Apply gently to the ear and jaw to ease muscle tension.

Gentle facial exercises: Start when comfortable to maintain muscle tone.

Hydration and nutrition: Eat soft, nutritious foods if chewing or swallowing is difficult.

Skin care: Keep blisters clean and dry; avoid picking to prevent infection.

Sleep hygiene: Elevate your head slightly to reduce ear pressure.

Coping with Shingles Pain in the Ear

Managing ear pain directly can make a big difference:

Over-the-counter analgesics—taken regularly as directed—can reduce baseline pain.

For breakthrough pain, ask your doctor about prescription nerve modulators.

Warm saltwater gargles may soothe any throat involvement.

Relaxation techniques (deep breathing, guided imagery) can help lower pain perception.

Avoid loud noises or pressure changes (air travel, diving) until symptoms resolve.

Once acute symptoms improve, rehabilitation can aid full recovery:

Facial muscle retraining: Therapists guide you through specific movements to restore symmetry.

Balance therapy: If you experienced vertigo, exercises can retrain your vestibular system.

Manual techniques: Gentle massage may relieve residual muscle tightness.

Engage a licensed physical or occupational therapist with experience in facial nerve recovery.

Recovery Timeline and Outlook

With early treatment, many people begin improving within 1–2 weeks.

Facial movement often returns over 3–6 months, though some changes can last longer.

Post-herpetic neuralgia (persistent pain) can occur, especially in older adults.

Hearing loss or tinnitus may resolve but sometimes requires audiology support.

Follow-up with your healthcare provider ensures you're on track and helps manage any lingering issues.

When to Seek Immediate Medical Attention

While most cases improve, watch for:

Sudden worsening of facial paralysis

Severe or spreading rash beyond the ear

High fever, confusion, or headache suggesting deeper infection

Signs of bacterial skin infection (increased redness, swelling, pus)

Persistent, severe vertigo leading to falls or nausea

If you experience any of these, speak to a doctor or go to the emergency department right away.
