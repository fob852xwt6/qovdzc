What is Ozempic?

Released in 2017, Ozempic was originally created as a treatment for type 2 diabetes. It's a weekly injectable medication approved by the FDA. It's active ingredient, semaglutide, is known as a GLP-1 agonist, meaning it stimulates the glucagon-like peptide-1 hormone, says Fatima Cody Stanford, M.D., M.P.H., obesity expert at Harvard University and Men's Health advisor. This hormone controls your body’s response to insulin and help reduce appetite by slowing the emptying of the stomach, as well as acting on the appetite center in the brain. This helps better manage blood sugar in diabetes patients.

This mechanism of use also causes you to feel fuller, longer, after eating. Because of this, Ozempic has gained its recent fame as a weight loss mechanism. It's becoming more common for doctors to prescribe this medication to those without diabetes as an anti-obesity medication, but it is only FDA approved for the treatment of diabetes.

Side Effects

Medications effect everyone differently. Maybe you've tried Ozempic in the past and have had negative adverse reactions to the medication. Possible side effects include vision problems, inflammation of the pancreas, nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, stomach pain, and constipation. Talk to your doctor if you've recently experienced severe side effects.

You're Not Responding to the Medication

If you're taking Ozempic for weight loss, it's possible for some people to not respond to the medication. "It is not clear why someone would respond it versus another. I presume that some people don’t have disregulation of the primary pathway of the brain that involves food regulation," says Cody Stanford.

You Hate Needles

Ozempic is an injectable medication—meaning it's a self-administered shot that you apply to your stomach, thigh, or inner arm. If you hate needles, or just really hate sticking yourself, you may be looking for something you can take orally.

Your Family History Doesn't Allow You To Take Ozempic

There are a few diseases that you can't have if you were to go on Ozempic. Those who have, or have a family history of, multiple endocrine neoplasia type two, medullary thyroid cancer, or pancreatitis should not go on any form of semaglutide, Cody Stanford says.

For Diabetes

Mounjaro

Mounjaro may be one of the better known alternatives to Ozempic. Approved by the FDA for both diabetic treatment and weight loss, this medication has two mechanisms of action. It's active ingredient, tirzepatide, effects both the GLP-1 and GIP receptors in your body—both responsible for stimulating insulin secretion. Some research suggests Mounjaro may be more effective than Ozempic at lowering blood sugar levels, Cody Stanford says.

Rybelsus

Rybelsus is also a GLP-1 medication, containing semaglutide, so it works under the same mechanism as Ozempic. The only difference between the two is its administration. Rybelsus is an oral medication, taken by mouth once a day, making it a potential contender for those who dislike needles.

Trulicity

Trulicity is another injectable medication that is self-administered weekly. This medication is also a GLP-1 agonist, with the active ingredient dulaglutide.

Common side effects include nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and fatigue. More serious symptoms include hypoglycemia, inflammation of the pancreas, acute kidney problems, changes in vision, gallbladder issues, and stomach problems.

Non-Medication Weight Loss Alternatives

While these medications sound promising in helping weight-management, they're not a cure-all. You'll still need to consume a healthy balanced diet and exercise to recommended amount for the medications to work properly. Plus, you're at risk of losing muscle mass if you stop strength training while on these medications.

Plus, the weight isn't likely to stay off after you finish the medication. Research has found significant weight regain after stopping the usage of semaglutide. There are several ways to lose weight that doesn't involve the use of prescription medications.
