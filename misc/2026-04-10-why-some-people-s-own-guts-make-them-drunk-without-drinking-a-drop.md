AT FIRST GLANCE, the ability to get drunk without ever drinking alcohol might sound like a dream. But for the people actually living with auto-brewery syndrome, it's more of a nightmare.

Imagine you're pulled over and the breathalyzer deems you too intoxicated to drive. How can you insist you never drank a sip of alcohol? How do you convince your child's teacher a drop of liquor never passed your lips when you're stumbling to pick them up?

There have been fewer than a hundred documented cases of auto-brewery syndrome, though scientists say the number is likely higher since the condition is hard to diagnose. It's been hard to treat too, but a recent study published in Nature Microbiology suggests this may change. The findings suggest scientists have been looking at the right place, but the wrong source all along.

How Some People Brew Alcohol in Their Guts

Normally, the human body creates small amounts of ethanol (an alcohol that enters the bloodstream) when it breaks down carbs. Gut bacteria and fungi are usually the alcohol-producing culprits. But the reason everyone isn't tipsy after eating a slice of bread is because our metabolism clears the ethanol before it enters the bloodstream.

For people with auto-brewery syndrome, that's not the case. There's an overgrowth of those alcohol-producing bacteria, causing a production of ethanol at a rate faster than your body can clear it. When it reaches the bloodstream, it can give you the feeling of being drunk. In some situations, the problem is metabolic. Some people have genetic variations or liver conditions that render your body unable to metabolize alcohol in your gut.

How Auto-Brewery Syndrome Used to be Treated

Doctors used to think that auto-brewery syndrome was caused by yeast in the gut and gave patients anti-fungal medications to try to treat it as a result. It's why the blanket recommendation is to stay away from carbs and eat more gut-friendly foods, since fungi is thought to be the culprit behind auto-brewery syndrome.

“But our study suggests that this is caused by bacteria in the gut,” Hohmann explains. “It’s very hard to treat this. Sometimes antibiotics work; Sometimes it’s a fecal transplant.”

What Did the Study Find?

The researchers got down and dirty. They swabbed poop samples from 22 people diagnosed with auto-brewery syndrome having an active flare-up along with 21 household partners without the condition. (People who share a household, eat the same foods, and live in similar hygienic conditions may share gut microbes). To make sure they nailed the unique alcohol-producing germs, the researchers also took poop samples of 22 people without the condition and not living in the same household.

First, the obvious. People with auto-brewery syndrome had significantly more ethanol in their system.

Antibiotic treatment helped to lower ethanol levels. One person with auto-brewery syndrome was given a poop transplant to introduce "good" germs into their gut. The researchers observed improvements in their symptoms which correlated with a restructuring of their microbiome.

What are the germs responsible for making the gut a distillery? The researchers identified a type of gram-negative bacteria called Proteobacteria. These include Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae. People with auto-brewery syndrome also had an enrichment of genes involved in ethanol production.

What This Means For You

Now, the researchers are doing a trial for eight patients with auto-brewery syndrome who are being treated with fecal-microbiota transplants. “This is ongoing, but hopefully it will show that fecal-microbiota transplants are safe and tolerable in these patients,” says Schnabl. “Our goal is really to try to find a good treatment for these patients.”
