Explanation

If your joints feel like they're grinding with every step — sharp, hot, stiff, or swollen — you're not imagining it. That "glass in the joints" sensation is often caused by inflammation, your body's internal fire alarm. When that alarm doesn't shut off, pain follows.

One medication doctors commonly use to calm that fire is diclofenac. But how does it work? Is it safe? And is it the right choice for you?

Let's break it down clearly and honestly — based on credible medical evidence and clinical practice.

Why Do Joints Feel Hot, Swollen, and Painful?

Joint pain often comes down to inflammation. When tissue is irritated — from arthritis, injury, or overuse — your immune system releases chemicals called prostaglandins. These substances:

Increase blood flow

Cause swelling

Trigger pain signals

Create warmth and redness

That's why inflamed joints may feel:

Hot

Swollen

Stiff

Tender

Difficult to move

What Is Diclofenac?

Diclofenac is a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID). It has been widely used for decades and is available by prescription and, in some forms, over the counter.

Doctors prescribe diclofenac to treat:

Ankylosing spondylitis

Acute muscle and joint injuries

Migraine attacks

Gout flares

Post-surgical pain

It comes in several forms:

Oral tablets or capsules

Extended-release tablets

Topical gel (applied to skin)

Topical solution

Patches

Injectable formulations (in medical settings)

Each form works slightly differently in terms of how much medication enters your bloodstream.

How Diclofenac Quells the "Internal Fire"

Here's the science in simple terms.

Diclofenac blocks enzymes called COX-1 and COX-2 (cyclooxygenase enzymes). These enzymes are responsible for producing prostaglandins — the chemicals that cause inflammation and pain.

When diclofenac blocks these enzymes:

Prostaglandin production drops

Swelling decreases

Pain signals reduce

Joint stiffness improves

In short: less inflammation = less pain.

Diclofenac is often considered one of the more potent NSAIDs, especially for inflammatory joint pain. Clinical studies have shown it to be effective for both osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis, and topical diclofenac has strong evidence for knee and hand arthritis.

Topical vs Oral Diclofenac: What's the Difference?

✅ Topical Diclofenac (Gel or Patch)

Applied directly to the painful joint.

Benefits:

Lower systemic absorption

Lower risk of stomach irritation

Fewer cardiovascular risks compared to oral forms

Good for knees, hands, elbows, and ankles

Best for:

Localized osteoarthritis

Mild to moderate joint pain

✅ Oral Diclofenac (Pill or Capsule)

Works throughout the body.

Stronger overall anti-inflammatory effect

Better for multiple painful joints

Helpful for inflammatory arthritis

Risks are higher because more medication circulates through the bloodstream.

Your doctor will help determine which form fits your condition and risk profile.

What Diclofenac Can (and Can't) Do

Let's be realistic.

✅ Diclofenac Can:

Reduce pain

Improve joint movement

Decrease swelling

Help you stay active

Improve quality of life

❌ Diclofenac Cannot:

Cure arthritis

Repair cartilage damage

Reverse joint degeneration

Replace physical therapy or lifestyle changes

It's a powerful tool — but not a standalone fix.

Safety: What You Need to Know

Diclofenac is effective, but it's not risk-free. This isn't meant to alarm you — just to inform you.

Potential Risks Include:

1. Stomach and Intestinal Problems

Ulcers

Bleeding

Irritation

Heartburn

Risk increases if you:

Are over 65

Take blood thinners

Use steroids

Have a history of ulcers

2. Cardiovascular Risk

Oral diclofenac may increase the risk of:

Heart attack

Stroke

This risk is higher if you:

Have heart disease

Have high blood pressure

Smoke

Have diabetes

Use high doses long term

3. Kidney Effects

NSAIDs can reduce kidney blood flow, especially in:

Older adults

People with kidney disease

Those who are dehydrated

4. Liver Enzyme Elevation

Rare but possible — doctors may monitor liver function for long-term users.

Who Should Be Cautious with Diclofenac?

Speak to a doctor before using diclofenac if you have:

Heart disease

History of stroke

Stomach ulcers

Kidney disease

Liver disease

High blood pressure

Are pregnant (especially third trimester)

If you experience:

Chest pain

Sudden weakness

Black or bloody stools

Severe stomach pain

Shortness of breath

Seek immediate medical care.

Use the lowest effective dose

When Joint Pain Is More Than "Just Arthritis"

Do not ignore symptoms such as:

Sudden severe swelling

Fever with joint pain

Inability to bear weight

One very red, extremely painful joint

Pain after injury with deformity

These could signal infection, gout, fracture, or other urgent conditions. If something feels significantly different from your usual pain, speak to a doctor promptly.

The Bottom Line on Diclofenac

Diclofenac is a powerful, evidence-based medication that reduces inflammation and pain by blocking the body's "internal fire" chemicals.

For many people, it:

Improves mobility

Reduces swelling

Makes daily life manageable again

But it must be used wisely.

The safest approach is:

Correct diagnosis

Lowest effective dose

Shortest necessary duration

Ongoing medical guidance
